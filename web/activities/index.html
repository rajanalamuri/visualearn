Paper activities

Every lesson ends with something to do away from the screen. Pick a level, then print.

Euclid's GCD Starter

Squares from a sheet

You need Squared paper, scissors and a pencil · Time 15 minutes

  1. Cut a rectangle 21 squares long and 13 squares wide out of squared paper.
  2. Fold the short side down to make the biggest square you can. Cut it off.
  3. Do the same with the strip that is left, and keep going.
  4. Write down the size of every square you cut. The last, smallest square is the GCD.
  5. Try again with 12 by 8. Can tiles of the smallest size fill the whole first rectangle?

Think about it. With 21 by 13 your squares were 13, 8, 5, 3, 2, 1, 1. Each number is the sum of the next two. Can you find a rectangle where every cut makes exactly one square?

Sieve of Eratosthenes Starter

The hundred square

You need Paper, a pencil and three or four colours · Time 20 minutes

  1. Draw a 10 by 10 grid and write the numbers 1 to 100, ten to a row.
  2. Shade 1. It is not prime.
  3. Circle 2. With one colour, shade every second number after it: 4, 6, 8 …
  4. Circle 3, the next number that is not shaded. With a new colour, shade every third number after it.
  5. Keep going with 5 and 7. Every number still not shaded is prime. Circle them all.

Think about it. You could stop after 7. Why do you never need to cross out with 11 when you go up to 100?

Counting sort Starter

Sort by counting

You need 20 playing cards, or numbers 0 to 5 on paper slips · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw 6 boxes labelled 0 to 5.
  2. Take each number in turn and put a tally mark in its box.
  3. Read the boxes from 0 to 5. Write each number as many times as it has tally marks.
  4. The list is sorted, and you never compared two numbers.

Think about it. Why would this stop working for numbers up to a billion?

Recursion Starter

Russian dolls

You need A set of nesting dolls (or 5 cups that stack), paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Open the biggest doll. Inside is a smaller one: open that. Keep going until the smallest, which holds nothing. That is the base case.
  2. Now close them back up from the smallest. Each time, you finish the job for that doll.
  3. Write 5! as 5 × 4!, 4! as 4 × 3!, and so on down to 1. Then work back up multiplying.
  4. Notice you needed to remember every unfinished doll on the way down.

Think about it. What would happen if the dolls never ended, with no smallest one?

Steps and loops Starter

Be the loop

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 5 minutes

  1. Write total = 0 on paper.
  2. Add 1, cross out the old total, write the new one. Then add 2, then 3, up to 6.
  3. You are the loop: same step, repeated, with a memory.

Think about it. How many steps would adding 1 to 1,000 take?

Strings: palindromes Starter

Mirror words

You need Letter tiles or paper slips · Time 10 minutes

  1. Spell RACECAR with tiles in a row.
  2. Point at the first and last tile. Do they match? Move both fingers inward.
  3. Try PENCIL and LEVEL. Stop at the first mismatch.

Think about it. How many comparisons does a word of 100 letters need at most?

Arrays Starter

The egg-box shuffle

You need An egg box (or 8 cups in a row), and 5 small objects · Time 10 minutes

  1. Put one object in each of the first five slots of the box. Number the slots 1 to 8.
  2. Ask a friend to name a slot. Point to it straight away. That is reading an array.
  3. Now squeeze a new object into slot 2. You must move the objects in slots 5, 4, 3 and 2 along, one at a time, starting at the end.
  4. Count your moves. Try inserting into slot 5 instead.

Think about it. Why must you start moving from the far end and not from slot 2? What happens if you start at the front?

Hash tables Starter

The seven-shelf shop

You need Paper, a pencil and 8 numbers between 10 and 99 · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw seven boxes in a row and write 0 to 6 above them. These are your shelves.
  2. Take a number. Divide it by 7 and write the remainder in your head. Write the number in that box.
  3. If the box already has numbers, write the new one beside them.
  4. When all eight are filed, ask a friend to pick one. Find it by checking only one box.

Think about it. Two numbers landed on the same shelf. Is that a mistake, or just something the table has to cope with? What if all eight landed on one shelf?

Graphs as pictures Starter

Draw the web

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw 6 dots and name them A to F. Join some with lines.
  2. Next to each dot, write the names of the dots it touches. That is its neighbour list.
  3. Pick a dot and check: does each neighbour list it back?
  4. Count the lines. Add up all the neighbour list lengths. What do you notice?

Think about it. Why is the total of all the list lengths exactly twice the number of lines?

Counting with hashes Starter

Tally the letters

You need A word on paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Write a word like SWISS... a longer one, like LETTERS.
  2. Make a list of letters and a tally column. For each letter in the word, find its row and add a tally.
  3. Now read the word again from the start. The first letter with a tally of 1 is the first letter that never repeats.

Think about it. Why is it better to keep a tally than to count the letter again every time you meet it?

Breadth-first search Starter

Ripple map

You need Squared paper and a pencil · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw a map on squared paper. Shade some squares as walls. Mark one square as home and one as school.
  2. Write 0 in the home square.
  3. Write 1 in every open square touching the 0. Then write 2 in every empty open square touching a 1, and so on, like ripples in a pond.
  4. Stop when you write a number in the school square. That number is the length of the shortest walk.
  5. Walk back from school, always stepping to a number one smaller, and draw the route.

Think about it. Why can there never be a shorter walk than the number you wrote in the school square?

Depth-first search Starter

Explore and back up

You need A maze drawn on paper, and a few coins · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a maze, or ask someone to draw one for you. Put your finger on the start.
  2. Each time you reach a fork, put a coin on it, then take a way you have not tried.
  3. At a dead end, go back to the last coin you put down and try another way from there.
  4. When every way from a coin has been tried, pick the coin up and go back to the one before.
  5. Keep going until you reach the exit.

Think about it. Your coins are a stack: the last one down is the first one up. Did you find the shortest way out, or just a way?

Modular arithmetic Starter

Be a clock

You need A paper clock with the numbers 0 to 6 (or 0 to 11) · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a clock with the numbers 0 to 6 round the edge.
  2. Put a coin on 5. Move it forward 4 steps, one number at a time, wrapping from 6 to 0.
  3. Where did it land? Check: (5 + 4) divided by 7, remainder.
  4. Try a few more sums.

Think about it. What is 100 hours after 3 o'clock on a 12-hour clock?

Binary search Starter

Guess my number

You need Two people. Nothing else. · Time 10 minutes

  1. One person thinks of a whole number from 1 to 100 and keeps it secret.
  2. The other person guesses. The answer is only ever "higher", "lower" or "yes!".
  3. Always guess the middle of what is left. Start with 50. If the answer is "higher", the next guess is 75.
  4. Count the guesses. Then swap roles.

Think about it. Seven guesses are always enough for 1 to 100. How many would you need for 1 to 1,000? For every person on Earth?

Linear search Starter

Find the ace

You need 10 playing cards with one ace, and a friend · Time 10 minutes

  1. Your friend shuffles the cards and lays them face down in a row.
  2. Turn over the cards one at a time, starting from the left.
  3. Make a tally mark for every card you turn. Stop when you find the ace.
  4. Swap roles and play five rounds. Write down how many cards you turned each time.

Think about it. What is the most cards you could ever turn? What is the average? What would it be with a whole deck of 52?

Bubble sort Starter

The bubble line-up

You need 4 to 8 people, or 8 playing cards · Time 10 minutes

  1. Stand in a line in any order.
  2. Start at the left end. Look at the first two people. If the taller one is on the left, they swap places.
  3. Take one step right and check the next two. Keep going to the end of the line.
  4. The tallest person is now at the right end. They are sorted, so they can sit down.
  5. Start again from the left. You are done when you walk the whole line and nobody swaps.

Think about it. How many times did the shortest person move? Why do small values travel left so slowly?

Insertion sort Starter

Sort your hand

You need 8 playing cards, face down in a pile · Time 10 minutes

  1. Pick up one card. A hand of one card is already sorted.
  2. Pick up the next card and slide it into the right place in your hand.
  3. Keep going, one card at a time, until the pile is gone.
  4. Now deal the cards out already in order and do it again. Notice how much faster it is.

Think about it. Why does insertion sort get so quick when the cards are nearly in order already?

Selection sort Starter

Find the smallest

You need 8 playing cards (ace counts as 1) · Time 10 minutes

  1. Lay the cards face up in a row, all mixed up.
  2. Touch each card one at a time and find the smallest.
  3. Move it down to start a new row underneath.
  4. Do the same with the cards that are left, until the top row is empty.

Think about it. Count every card you touch. With 8 cards it is always 28, however they start. Can you work out why?

Greedy choices Starter

Change maker

You need Coins or paper discs worth 25, 10, 5 and 1 (or 4, 3 and 1) · Time 10 minutes

  1. Pick an amount, like 63. Always take the biggest coin that still fits.
  2. Count your coins.
  3. Now try coins worth 4, 3 and 1 for the amount 6. Greedy takes 4 + 1 + 1. Can you do it with fewer?

Think about it. For which coins does "biggest first" always give the fewest coins?

Amortised cost Builder

The doubling shelf

You need Paper cups and small objects · Time 10 minutes

  1. Start with a row of 1 cup. Put an object in.
  2. When the row is full and you want to add another, lay out a row twice as long and carry every object across. Count each carry.
  3. Add 9 objects in all. Count every placement and every carry.
  4. Divide the total by 9.

Think about it. Why is it better to double the space than to add just one more cup each time?

Best, worst and average case Builder

Luck of the draw

You need 8 cards face down in a row, and a friend · Time 10 minutes

  1. Lay 8 cards in a row, face down. Pick a target card.
  2. Open from the left until you find it. Count the cards opened.
  3. Repeat 8 times with the target in a different place each time (first, second, … last).
  4. Add up the counts and divide by 8. That is the average case.

Think about it. Why do we usually plan for the worst case, even though it is the least likely?

Cycle detection Builder

Tortoise and hare

You need A drawing of 7 dots joined by arrows, and two coins · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw 7 dots with arrows between them. Make the last arrow go back to an earlier dot, or leave it out.
  2. Put two coins on the first dot. Each turn, move one coin one arrow and the other coin two arrows.
  3. If the coins meet, there is a loop. If one coin runs off the end, there is not.

Think about it. Why must the fast coin catch the slow one if there is a loop?

Deques Builder

Candidates in a queue

You need 9 number cards and a strip of paper · Time 15 minutes

  1. Lay 9 cards in a row. We want the biggest in every 3 in a row.
  2. Keep a short queue of candidates. A new card removes any smaller cards from the back, then joins the back.
  3. If the front card has slid out of the window, remove it.
  4. The front card is the biggest in the window. Write it down.

Think about it. Why can a smaller card behind a bigger one never be the biggest again?

Invariants Builder

Keep the promise

You need A hand of 6 playing cards · Time 10 minutes

  1. Hold the cards in a row. The first card on its own is already "sorted".
  2. Take the next card and slide it left into place among the cards already sorted.
  3. After each card, check: is everything to the left of my finger in order?
  4. When your finger reaches the end, all cards are in order.

Think about it. What would break if you inserted a card into the wrong place once?

Linked lists: reverse Builder

Turn the arrows

You need 5 cards in a row and string or pencil arrows · Time 10 minutes

  1. Lay 5 cards in a row with an arrow from each card to the next.
  2. Start at the first card. Remember where its arrow pointed, then turn its arrow to point backward.
  3. Move to the card you remembered and repeat.
  4. When you finish, the chain runs the other way.

Think about it. What goes wrong if you turn an arrow before remembering where it pointed?

Matrices and grids Builder

Spiral walk

You need Squared paper (3 rows by 4 columns) and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a grid 4 across and 3 down and write 1 to 12 in order.
  2. Start in the top-left. Go right until you cannot, then down, then left, then up.
  3. Cross out squares as you visit them. Turn whenever the next square is crossed out or is off the grid.
  4. Write the numbers in the order you visited them.

Think about it. How does the spiral know when it has finished?

Prefix sums Builder

Running total

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Write 8 numbers in a row. Under them, write a running total: each box is the box before plus the number above.
  2. Pick two positions. The sum between them is the running total at the end minus the running total just before the start.
  3. Check by adding the numbers one by one. They agree.

Think about it. If you had to answer 1000 questions about the same row, why is the running total worth building first?

Recursion: the call tree Builder

Draw the calls

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw fib(5) at the top. Under it draw its two calls, fib(4) and fib(3). Keep drawing each call's two calls until you reach fib(1) or fib(0).
  2. Count the circles. How many say fib(2)? How many say fib(3)?
  3. Write the answer in each circle from the bottom up.
  4. Now think: how could you avoid answering fib(2) more than once?

Think about it. If fib(5) needs 15 calls, roughly how many would fib(10) need? What would writing answers down save?

Sliding window Builder

Slide the frame

You need A strip of squared paper with numbers, and a card with a 3-square window cut out · Time 10 minutes

  1. Write 9 numbers in a row on the strip.
  2. Put the window over the first 3 and add them.
  3. Slide the window one place. Do not add again: add the new number, take away the one that left.
  4. Keep the best total. Slide to the end.

Think about it. How many additions does each slide need, compared with adding all 3 from scratch? What if the window were 100 wide?

Space and time Builder

The answer sheet

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 15 minutes

  1. Pick 5 numbers up to 30. For each, test whether it is prime by dividing by 2, 3, 4, 5 and counting your divisions.
  2. Now write a list of all the primes up to 30 once (your table).
  3. Check the same 5 numbers by looking in the table. Count your looks.
  4. Which was faster for 5 questions? For 5,000?

Think about it. When is it not worth building the table?

Two pointers Builder

Pair up

You need Number cards in order, and a target number · Time 10 minutes

  1. Lay 8 number cards face up in order, smallest on the left.
  2. Pick a target. Put one finger on the first card and one on the last.
  3. Add the two cards. Too small? Move the left finger right. Too big? Move the right finger left.
  4. Stop when they add to the target, or when your fingers meet.

Think about it. Why can you safely ignore a card once its finger has moved past it?

Binary search trees Builder

The family tree of numbers

You need Paper, a pencil and 7 numbers · Time 15 minutes

  1. Write the first number at the top of the page. That is the root.
  2. For each next number, start at the root. If it is smaller, move down-left. If it is bigger, move down-right.
  3. When you reach an empty space, write the number there and draw a line to its parent.
  4. Pick a number and find it by starting at the top again. Count your steps.

Think about it. What would the tree look like if you added the numbers already in order, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7? How would that change the search?

Counting walks Builder

Count the paths

You need Squared paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a grid of 4 rows and 5 columns. Shade 3 squares as blocked, but not the start or the finish.
  2. Write 1 in the top-left square. Write 0 in every blocked square.
  3. Fill each remaining square, row by row, with the number above it plus the number to its left.
  4. Read the bottom-right square. That is how many different walks there are.

Think about it. What happens to the numbers just after a blocked square? How would you count walks if you could also move diagonally?

Fewest coins Builder

The coin table

You need Paper, a pencil and three kinds of coins, such as 1, 4 and 5 (real or drawn) · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw a row of 11 boxes, numbered 0 to 10. Write 0 in box 0.
  2. For box 1: take away each coin that fits, look at the box that is left, and add 1. Write the smallest.
  3. Do the same for box 2, box 3, and on up to box 10. Always look back at boxes you have already filled.
  4. Check box 8. Is it what you get by grabbing the biggest coin first?

Think about it. The table made every answer from smaller answers. Why is that quicker than trying every possible handful of coins from scratch?

Connected components Builder

Count the islands

You need A drawing of 9 dots and some lines, and 3 coloured pencils · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw 9 dots and join some. Leave some dots cut off from others.
  2. Pick a dot and colour it. Then colour every dot you can reach by following lines.
  3. Pick an uncoloured dot with a new colour and repeat.
  4. The number of colours you used is the number of islands.

Think about it. Can two dots of different colours ever share a line?

Cycle detection in graphs Builder

Find the loop

You need A drawing of 6 dots and 5 or 6 lines, and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw 6 dots joined by 5 lines so every dot is reachable. Walk from dot A, marking each dot you reach.
  2. Add one more line between two dots that are already joined by a path.
  3. Walk again. When a line leads to a marked dot (that you did not just come from), you have found the loop.

Think about it. Why does a connected graph with n dots and n − 1 lines never have a cycle?

Topological sort Builder

Getting ready

You need Cards for 6 jobs, and arrows drawn between them · Time 15 minutes

  1. Write jobs on cards: Wake, Wash, Dress, Eat, Pack, Go. Draw an arrow from each job to one that must come after it.
  2. Next to each card write how many arrows point into it.
  3. Do a card with 0, write the order down, and cross out the arrows leaving it.
  4. Repeat until all cards are done.

Think about it. Can there be more than one valid order? What if two jobs wait for each other?

Two-colouring (bipartite) Builder

Two crayons

You need A drawing of a loop of dots, and 2 crayons · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a loop of 4 dots and join them in a ring. Colour one dot red.
  2. Colour its neighbours blue, then their neighbours red, going round the ring.
  3. Does it work? Now try a loop of 5.

Think about it. Why does an odd-length loop always fail?

Caches (LRU) Builder

The small desk

You need 3 spaces on a desk, and cards A to D · Time 10 minutes

  1. A desk fits only 3 cards. Someone asks for cards in a list like A B C A D B.
  2. If the card is on the desk, slide it to the newest end. If not, fetch it. If the desk is full, send away the card that has been there untouched the longest.
  3. Count hits (already on the desk) and misses.

Think about it. Why is "least recently used" a good guess for what you will not need next?

Compression (Huffman) Builder

Make a code

You need Paper, a pencil and a short message · Time 20 minutes

  1. Write a short message and count each letter.
  2. Write each letter with its count. Cross out the two smallest and replace them with one item that has their total.
  3. Repeat until one item is left. You have drawn a tree.
  4. Label left branches 0 and right branches 1. Each letter's code is the path from the top to it.

Think about it. Why does giving the common letters shorter codes make the whole message shorter?

Pattern matching Builder

Slide the word

You need A strip of paper with a short word, and a longer text on paper · Time 10 minutes

  1. Write a text of 12 letters from A, B and C. Write a pattern of 3 letters on a paper strip.
  2. Place the strip under the start of the text. Do all three letters match? Tick or cross.
  3. Slide the strip one place right and repeat until the end.
  4. Mark every place where all three letters matched.

Think about it. How many comparisons would you need to find a 100-letter pattern in a million-letter text, if every place nearly matched?

Counting (nCr) Builder

Build the triangle

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. Write 1 at the top. Below it write 1 1. Then 1 2 1. Each new row starts and ends with 1.
  2. Each number in the middle is the two numbers above it added up.
  3. Go to row 5 and read place 2: that is the number of ways to pick 2 things from 5.
  4. Check by listing the pairs from A, B, C, D, E.

Think about it. Why does choosing 2 from 5 give the same number as choosing 3 from 5?

Fast powers Builder

Square and multiply

You need Paper and a calculator (optional) · Time 15 minutes

  1. Write 13 in binary: 1101.
  2. Start with 1. For the first bit (1): square, then multiply by 3. That gives 3.
  3. Next bit (1): square → 9, multiply by 3 → 27. Next bit (0): just square → 729. Last bit (1): square, then multiply by 3.
  4. Count the multiplications and compare with 12.

Think about it. How many multiplications does a power of one million need this way?

Line sweep Builder

Sweep the timeline

You need Paper strips marking meetings on a timeline from 0 to 12 · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw 6 meetings as bars along a timeline.
  2. Write a + at each start and a − at each end, in order along the timeline.
  3. Walk along the line keeping a count: add 1 at a +, subtract 1 at a −.
  4. The biggest count is the number of rooms you need.

Think about it. Why can you ignore all the times when nothing starts or ends?

Dijkstra's shortest path Builder

Shortest way to school

You need Paper, a pencil and a ruler · Time 20 minutes

  1. Draw six dots as towns and join them with roads. Write a length on each road, 1 to 9.
  2. Write 0 next to Home and a question mark next to every other town.
  3. Circle the unsettled town with the smallest number. Its number is final.
  4. For each road leaving it, add the road length to its number. If that beats the neighbour's number, cross it out and write the smaller one.
  5. Repeat until School is circled. Trace the route back by following the roads that gave each final number.

Think about it. Why is it safe to say a town's number is final the moment it is the smallest? What would go wrong if a road could have a negative length?

Merge sort Builder

Split and merge

You need 8 playing cards and a table · Time 15 minutes

  1. Split the cards into 2 piles of 4. Split those into 4 piles of 2. Split again into 8 single cards.
  2. Merge the single cards in pairs, smaller card on top.
  3. Merge two pairs into a sorted pile of 4. Only look at the top card of each pile and take the smaller one.
  4. Merge the two piles of 4 into one sorted pile of 8.

Think about it. When you merge, you only ever compare the top two cards. Why is that enough?

Quick sort Builder

The pivot game

You need 8 to 10 playing cards · Time 10 minutes

  1. Mix the cards and pick the last one as the pivot. Put it in the middle of the table.
  2. Every card smaller than the pivot goes in a pile on its left. Every other card goes on its right.
  3. The pivot is now in its final place. Leave it there.
  4. Play the same game with the left pile, then the right pile, until every pile has one card.

Think about it. What happens if the pivot is always the biggest card? Try it, and count how many times you compare.

Approximation Builder

Guard the roads

You need Paper and a pencil, and a small map of 6 towns and 7 roads · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw 6 towns and 7 roads. A guard in a town protects every road that touches it.
  2. Pick any unguarded road. Put guards at both its ends. Cross out every road they now protect.
  3. Repeat until all roads are crossed out. Count the guards.
  4. Try to find a smaller set by thinking. Is yours less than half of what the quick method used?

Think about it. Why can the best possible answer never use fewer than half as many guards as this quick method?

Easy and hard problems (P vs NP) Builder

Doubling sets

You need Paper and a pencil · Time 10 minutes

  1. List all the sets you can make from the numbers {1}: there are 2 (empty, and {1}).
  2. Now from {1, 2}: 4 sets. From {1, 2, 3}: 8. List them all.
  3. Check a guess like {1, 3} with one addition. How many sets would you list for 20 numbers?

Think about it. If a computer could check a billion sets a second, how long would 60 numbers take?

Game theory (minimax) Builder

Play the stones

You need 13 coins or counters and a friend · Time 15 minutes

  1. Put 13 coins in a pile. Take turns removing 1, 2 or 3. Whoever takes the last coin wins.
  2. Play a few games. Try to find a pile size that is always bad for whoever is about to move.
  3. Work out why multiples of 4 are the losing piles: whatever you take, your friend can take enough to leave another multiple of 4.

Think about it. If you go first with 13 coins, how many should you take?

Randomised algorithms Builder

Dart board

You need Squared paper (10 by 10), a dice or coin to choose squares, and a ruler · Time 20 minutes

  1. Draw a 10 by 10 grid and a quarter-circle of radius 10 from the bottom-left corner.
  2. Pick 20 random squares (roll a 10-sided dice twice, or draw numbers from a hat). Mark each one.
  3. Count how many fall inside the curve.
  4. Multiply that count by 4 and divide by 20. How close to 3.14 is it?

Think about it. What happens to your answer if you throw 1000 darts instead of 20?

Heap sort Builder

Pyramid sort

You need Number cards (7) and a pyramid drawn on paper · Time 15 minutes

  1. Fill the pyramid with the cards, then fix it so every card is bigger than the cards below it.
  2. Swap the top card with the last card in the pyramid. Set that card aside: it is the biggest.
  3. Sink the new top card down until the pyramid is in order again.
  4. Keep going. The cards you set aside, read backward, are sorted.

Think about it. Why is the biggest card always on top of a max-heap?

Heaps and priority queues Builder

The family pyramid

You need Number cards and a pyramid drawn on paper · Time 15 minutes

  1. Draw a pyramid: 1 box on top, 2 below, 4 below those. Boxes are filled left to right.
  2. Place each new card in the next empty box. If it is smaller than the card above it, swap them, and repeat.
  3. After 7 cards, the smallest is on top. Take it. Put the last card on top and sink it by swapping with its smaller child.
  4. The new top is the second smallest.

Think about it. Where in the pyramid can the third smallest number be?

Inorder traversal Builder

Walk the tree

You need A tree drawn on paper with 7 numbers, and a finger · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a tree with 7 numbers: one at the top, and up to two below each.
  2. Follow the rule: left, node, right. Say each number out loud as you "visit" it.
  3. Write the numbers in the order you said them.
  4. Try the other three orders on the same tree. Are the lists the same?

Think about it. Which order gives a sorted list on a search tree?

Postorder traversal Builder

Walk the tree

You need A tree drawn on paper with 7 numbers, and a finger · Time 10 minutes

  1. Draw a tree with 7 numbers: one at the top, and up to two below each.
  2. Follow the rule: left, right, node. Say each number out loud as you "visit" it.

Waiting in line
